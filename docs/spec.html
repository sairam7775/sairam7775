What changed from draft 2

Eight further decisions closed — D9–D16 in §02 — covering the schema questions that had to be settled before a migration could be written. Two of them changed the design rather than filling in a blank:

Budget moved from the trip to the city. "Varies by city" means a ryokan night in Hakone must not make the Tokyo days score as overspending, so budget_band lives on trip_cities.

Negative preferences had nowhere to live. Places say who should skip them; nothing said what a traveller refuses outright. New user_preferences.excludes[], applied as a hard filter in scheduler step 2 — "no beaches" means never, not rarely.

The first deep cities changed from Tokyo + Kyoto to the Kansai–Sanyo corridor (§16), on the owner's actual interests rather than an assumption about first-timers.

Renamed from Meguri to Bento. The audience is people who have never been to Japan, and a name they cannot pronounce or spell is a name they cannot recommend. Bento is a Japanese word English already borrowed — and the metaphor is exact: carefully chosen things, arranged in compartments, that fit together in one box.

Every yen figure, duration and booking window here is a seed value written to show the shape of the data. Each becomes a row carrying last_verified and verified_by, and nothing reaches a user until a human has signed it off (§10).
§01

What this actually is

It reads as one chat box, but it is three products stacked, and they are not equally hard. Draft 1 called the middle layer a constraint engine and treated it as plumbing. It is not plumbing — it is the product, and it is ours to write.

LAYER 1

Bento Man

The guide the traveller talks to. Understands what kind of trip this is, asks good follow-up questions, and explains the plan in language a first-timer can act on. Voice defined in §04.

Rented — a language model
LAYER 2

The recommender & scheduler

Scores every place against this traveller, then builds days that respect opening hours, closures, travel time and pace. Deterministic, testable, explainable.

Ours — §05, the moat
LAYER 3

The trip vault

Bookings the traveller uploads, matched to nights and legs, with a detector that names what is still missing in plain English.

Ours — plain code, drives retention

The single product bet

Anyone can generate a Japan itinerary. Almost nobody generates one where the travel times are real, the constraints hold, and the plan admits what it does not know. We compete on being correct and candid — and correctness lives in code we own, not in model weights we rent.

§03

The traveller's line

A first-timer arrives with these questions in this order. Each stop is a screen, a piece of stored state, and a point where the plan can be corrected.

BN-01

Account

TravellerSigns up with email or Google. Nothing else asked yet.
SystemCreates a user and an empty trip. Sign-up exists to hold confirmation numbers later, so it is real auth with row-level isolation from the first migration.
BN-02

Shape of the trip

TravellerDates, nights available, budget band, pace, interests, group, mobility.
SystemSeven questions maximum, conversational, skippable. These become the weights the recommender scores against.
BN-03

Route — cities and nights

Traveller"Is five cities in nine days mad?" It is. They need telling.
SystemProposes cities with nights allocated and a reason each, and refuses over-packed routes out loud with the arithmetic shown. Coverage tier stated per city.
BN-04

Days — the itinerary

TravellerDrags, deletes, pins. Says "too many temples."
SystemThe scheduler builds ordered days; redundancy scoring means the fourth temple loses to something else without being told. Re-plans around pins (§08).
BN-05

Logistics verdicts

Traveller"Do I buy a JR Pass? How much cash?"
SystemAnswers against their own legs, with the arithmetic. Usually the answer on the pass is no (§07).
BN-06

Bookings and gaps

TravellerUploads confirmations as they book. Nothing is fetched from airline or hotel sites.
SystemMatches each booking to a night or a leg and names what is missing, as a coverage ribbon plus plain sentences (§11).
BN-07

On the ground

TravellerAt a ticket gate, one bar of signal, needs today's plan and a booking reference.
SystemToday view, offline-capable, printable. All planning data is local, so this works by construction rather than by effort.
§04

Division of labour

D8 settled the question that shapes everything else: we own the reasoning and rent the language. The practical test for where something belongs is whether being wrong is visible. A badly phrased sentence is obvious and harmless. A silently dropped constraint looks like a working plan and strands someone. Everything in the second category belongs in code.

The engine decides

Deterministic · unit-tested · free to run
  • Which places are candidates for this traveller
  • What order they go in and at what times
  • Whether two places conflict in a day
  • Travel time, mode, fare and platform
  • Whether a day is over-packed
  • What is missing from the bookings
  • Every number shown anywhere in the product

The model decides

Probabilistic · evaluated · rented
  • What "somewhere quiet, not too touristy" means as tags
  • Which follow-up question is worth asking next
  • How to phrase a reason the engine already computed
  • How to read an uploaded booking confirmation
  • When to say it does not know

The rule that keeps this honest

The model may never state a fact it was not handed. Times, fares, hours and durations come from the engine and are passed into the prompt as data. If the engine did not compute it, the product does not claim it.

Bento Man — the voice

The assistant has a name because a traveller should know who they are asking. It is not a mascot. The character is a local friend who knows the place and will tell you the truth about it — which is the only personality compatible with a product whose entire premise is candour.

Sounds like

write the system prompt to these
  • "Skip the Skytree. The Metro Government Building observatory is free and the view is better."
  • "I don't know Takayama well enough yet to plan your days there."
  • "That's four cities in six days. You'd spend a third of it on trains — here's the arithmetic."
  • "Fushimi Inari at 6am is the one people remember. At 11am it's a queue."

Never sounds like

the failure modes
  • Enthusiasm as filler — no "Great choice!", no exclamation marks
  • Emoji inside plan content
  • Elaborate apology when something is wrong; say what happened and fix it
  • Confident prose where a fact is missing — the gap is the information
  • Flattery, or agreeing with a plan it can see does not work

Warmth comes from usefulness, not from tone

The temptation with a named assistant is to make it chirpy, and a chirpy guide delivering "you have nowhere to sleep on 14 April" reads as oblivious. Bento Man is warm the way a good local is warm — by telling you the thing you needed to know before you had to ask.

§05

The engine

This is the part that would be a fine-tuned model in a worse version of this product, and it should not be. It is a scoring function and a scheduler — classical, explainable, and testable against fixtures. It also means the reason shown on every itinerary item is true by construction: it is assembled from the terms that actually moved the score, not written by a model after the fact.

The recommender — scoring a place for this traveller
// every term contributes one clause to the shown reason score(place | user, trip, day) = + 2.5 × interest_match tag overlap with user.interests 0–1 + 1.5 × signature significance for a first-timer, hand-set 0–1 + 1.0 × confidence verified 1.0 · draft 0.4 · stub excluded + 0.8 × season_fit koyo in Nov, sakura in Apr, festivals 0–1 − 2.0 × redundancy nth place of same category, decays 1/n − 1.5 × mobility_conflict place.skip_if ∩ user.constraints − 1.0 × budget_strain cost against user.budget_band − 0.7 × pace_strain duration against remaining day budget

Fig. 1 — weights are configuration, not constants. They are the first thing to tune against the eval set.

The scheduler — building one day
1 BUDGET pace → active minutes relaxed 360 · standard 480 · packed 600 2 FILTER drop on hard constraints — no scoring, no exceptions closed on this weekday · closed on this date · booking required and not booked · coverage_tier = stub · category ∈ user.excludes ("no beaches" means never) 3 ANCHOR seed with the highest-scoring place that has a best_window (Fushimi Inari at 07:00 anchors the morning) 4 GROW insert the candidate maximising Δscore − λ·Δtravel until the budget is spent or no marginal gain is positive 5 ORDER 2-opt the sequence to cut total transit time 6 TIME assign clock times from opening hours, transit legs and meal slots; carry slack forward explicitly 7 VALIDATE re-check every hard constraint on the finished day; on failure drop the offender and refill from step 4

Fig. 2 — greedy insertion with local search. Not optimal, and it does not need to be — it needs to be right, fast, and explainable.

Why this earns its place over asking a model

It runs in milliseconds and costs nothing. It is unit-testable, so "Arashiyama and Fushimi Inari in one afternoon" becomes a failing test rather than a customer complaint. It produces the same plan twice for the same input. And because every clause of the reason traces to a term in Fig. 1, the traveller can disagree with the reasoning, not just the result.

§06

Coverage — all of Japan, honestly

D2 set the target at every destination in Japan at full depth — roughly 2,000 records. That is months of curation, and waiting for it would mean no usable product until it was finished. So coverage is tiered and the tier is shown to the user and passed to the planner. The app covers all of Japan from day one; it simply tells the truth about how well.

TierContainsPlanner behaviourAt launch
deep 40+ places, every judgement field verified, transit graph built, booking lead times recorded Builds full day itineraries 4 cities
outline 10–20 places, factual fields verified, judgement fields partial Suggests places, will not claim a day plan is complete ~15 cities
stub Name, region, rail connection, coordinates. Nothing claimed. Says plainly it does not know this place well enough yet everything else

The failure this prevents

The alternative is silent thinness — a Takayama itinerary that looks exactly like the Kyoto one but is built from four records and a guess. That is the single most damaging thing this product could do, because it is invisible until someone is standing in Takayama with a bad plan. A stated tier makes thin coverage a feature of the interface rather than a lie in the output.

§07

What "local guide" means in the data

A guidebook says Fushimi Inari: famous for its torii gates. A local says go before eight or the first gates are a queue; the full summit loop is three hours and most people turn back at Yotsutsuji, which is fine. The second only exists if the schema has fields for it — and if a human has signed those fields off.

// places — one verified record, abridged { id: "kyt-fushimi-inari", name: "Fushimi Inari Taisha", name_ja: "伏見稲荷大社", city: "kyoto", coords: [34.9671, 135.7727], nearest_station: "JR Inari (Nara Line) · 2 min walk", // ── judgement fields · hidden until verified ──────── duration: { taste: "45m", // to the dense gate section and back typical: "1h30m", // up to Yotsutsuji viewpoint full: "3h" // summit loop, 233m climb, ~4km }, best_window: ["before 08:00", "after 17:30"], crowd_note: "10:00–16:00 the first 300 gates are a shuffling queue. Past Yotsutsuji it empties out almost entirely.", worth_it_if: ["shrines", "photography", "hiking", "early_riser"], skip_if: ["limited_mobility", "packed_schedule", "heat_sensitive"], signature: 0.9, // first-timer significance → Fig. 1 category: "shrine", // drives redundancy scoring cost_jpy: 0, booking: "none", closed_days: [], conflicts_with: ["kyt-arashiyama"], // opposite ends of the city // ── provenance · the honesty gate ────────────────── verification_status: "verified", // draft → hidden judgement fields verified_by: "sai", last_verified: "2026-09-14", sources: ["wikidata:Q311227", "official-site"] }

Fig. 3 — conflicts_with stops the scheduler pairing Arashiyama and Fushimi Inari; verification_status stops a drafted guess reaching a traveller.

Transit legs are described, not timed

"20 minutes" is useless to someone who has never used a Japanese station. The leg a traveller can follow reads: JR Nara Line from Kyoto Station, platform 8–10, 2 stops to Inari, 5 min, ¥150, IC card, no reservation — but skip the Rapid, it does not stop at Inari. That last clause is the difference between a plan and a guide.

§08

Feedback — diffs, not regeneration

The failure mode of every chat-based planner: the traveller spends an hour refining a plan, asks for one change, and everything regenerates. Their edits vanish. They leave.

So the itinerary is structured state in the database, never chat text. The model does not rewrite it — it calls the engine, and the engine returns a diff the traveller accepts or rejects one piece at a time.

// itinerary_items — two fields carry the whole design { id: "itm_8f21", day_id: "day_kyoto_2", order: 2, place_id: "kyt-fushimi-inari", start: "07:00", duration_min: 90, locked: true, // ← pinned. The scheduler treats it as fixed. reason: "Early start beats the 10am crush, and it puts you back at Kyoto Station by 09:30 for the Nara train.", reason_terms: ["best_window", "interest:shrines", "transit_fit"], // ← which Fig. 1 terms produced that sentence arrive_by: { from: "itm_8f20", mode: "rail", minutes: 12, detail: "JR Nara Line, Kyoto → Inari, 2 stops, 5 min, ¥150. Local only — the Rapid does not stop at Inari." } }

Fig. 4 — locked survives re-planning; reason_terms ties the sentence back to the scoring that produced it.

Security, since we hold booking references

A PNR plus a surname is often enough to view or cancel someone's flight. So: references encrypted at rest, never logged, never placed in a model prompt; storage buckets scoped per user; RLS on every table from the first migration; uploaded confirmations parsed in a separate model call with no tools attached, so a malicious pasted blob has nothing to steer.

§10

How 2,000 records get written

D6 settled this, and it is the largest single piece of work in the project. Hand-writing every record at the Fig. 3 quality bar is 500+ hours. The pipeline below brings that to roughly 150, without letting an unverified guess reach a traveller.

StageSourceProducesGate
1 · SkeletonWikidata, OpenStreetMap, Wikivoyage — free, openly licensed, attribution where requiredName, coordinates, category, prefecture, nearest stationstub immediately visible
2 · DraftA model drafts judgement fields from sources it is handedProposed duration, best_window, crowd_note, skip_ifhidden from users entirely
3 · VerifyA human reads, corrects, and signs off in the admin UIverified_by + last_verified stampedoutline or deep
4 · Re-verifyQueue prioritised by how often a place appears in real itinerariesRefreshed hours, prices, booking windowsEffort goes where users are

The one rule that makes this safe

A drafted judgement field is never shown to a traveller. Stage 2 output is invisible until a human has passed it through stage 3. Without this gate, the AI-assisted pipeline reintroduces exactly the hallucination risk the whole product exists to avoid — and it would do so at 2,000-record scale.

Refresh cadences — daily is the wrong answer for most of it

Holidays and festivalsannuallyPublished a year ahead
Closures and disruptionsneverDo not attempt. A cached app cannot know a line is down. Link to official status and say so.
§11

Gaps the traveller can understand

Bookings are only ever what the traveller uploads — nothing is fetched from airline or hotel sites. That removes OAuth, scraping and a large share of the security surface in one decision. What remains is pure logic over their own data, which means the gap detector needs no external anything.

The mental model is coverage, not a checklist. Three things must be continuously covered: every night needs a bed, every city transition needs a way to get there, every timed-entry place needs a ticket.

SeverityMeaningExample
blockingThe trip physically does not workNo accommodation, night of 14 April, Kyoto
closingStill possible, but not for longGhibli Museum tickets release in 6 days for your date
advisoryFine to leave, worth knowingNo dinner booked on your one free evening in Osaka

In the interface: a coverage ribbon down the trip — one bar per night, solid where covered, hatched where not — so the gap is visible before a word is read. Under it, plain sentences:

You have nowhere to sleep on the night of 14 April.

You arrive in Kyoto that afternoon and your next booking is the Osaka hotel on the 16th.

Not MISSING: accommodation_booking [day_4]. The sentence says what is wrong, where, and what it sits between — which is what makes it understandable rather than merely flagged.

§16

User zero

D5 made the owner the first real traveller, so onboarding is designed against an actual person rather than an invented persona. This profile also sets curation priority and becomes the first eval fixture — if the engine cannot plan a good trip for this profile, it does not work.

The profile

seeds user_preferences
  • Pace — standard, ~8 active hours a day
  • Crowds — avoids them, willing to start early. Weights scheduler step 3 toward early best_window anchors
  • Budget — varies by city, so the band lives on trip_cities
  • Interests — shrines & temples · history & castles · nature as scenery
  • Excludes — beaches, and nature that requires hiking. Hard filter, not a penalty

What it implies

why the first cities changed
  • Shrines, castles and low-effort scenery is a Kansai profile, not a Tokyo one
  • Osaka · Kyoto · Nara · Hiroshima go deep first (D15)
  • Miyajima folds into Hiroshima — Itsukushima is a shrine, and nobody visits one without the other
  • Himeji is a near-free addition: the best surviving castle in Japan, directly on the line between Osaka and Hiroshima
  • All four sit on one rail corridor, so a single transit graph serves them all

The distinction that broke the schema

"Nature but not hiking" is why D10 chose tags plus axes. A flat nature tag cannot separate Kenroku-en from the Kumano Kodo; the tag with a low energy axis can. And "no beaches" needed somewhere to live at all — hence excludes[]. A real profile found both holes in an afternoon; invented personas would not have.
